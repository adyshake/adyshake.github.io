title: The way I learn
---

Earlier in 2025, I had made the decision to move to the Bay Area. This involved finding another job in the tech industry, which, well, requires leetcoding, again. :sigh:

I remembered struggling with leetcode the last time I had to prepare for interviews in 2019 and I'd gotten so rusty that I'd basically have to start from scratch. In the beginning, it was okay. I was able to solve your basic leetcode easys and mediums. But then very quickly, I started forgetting the solutions for the problems I'd solved earlier, and would roughly take the same amount of time as if I hadn't seen the problem in a long time.

I wanted to fix this recall issue, so I decided to use Anki for this initially, which seemed like a bad fit to me at the time. Memorizing the solution to a whole leetcode problem felt quite off. As time went on, I structured the cards better, to be more atomic, to answer not the whole code but just the trick for the problem. I guess it also helped that I got a little dopamine boost each time I would complete my reviews every day and see my cards due at zero.

Then something strange started happening with practice. Whenever I'd solve newer problems, I was able to compose a solution from memory using those memorized solution chunks. I know this probably isn't news to most folks. But I think I'd forgotten this feeling after college. At work and generally in life, I'm rarely forcing myself to memorize things since the cost of retrieval is so low -- I can always "just look things up". But when concepts are really drilled in and internalized, the brain really doesn't stop thinking about them. It keeps connecting those concepts in new ways to other old ideas and new stuff you keep encountering every day.

Actually I did feel this joy of learning a while ago, when I'd finished Sekiro and Elden Ring. Both games require you to really master the timing for attacks, dodges and parries for different enemies and tie them all together. It forces you to slow down, recognize patterns and practice. And very rarely do you defeat bosses on the first try or day. It takes a bit of practice, then you sleep it off and then the next day, your brain just does it.

By using Anki I was eventually able to land another job soon enough and it felt bittersweet to stop using it. So then I just extended it to learn random stuff that I just encounter through the day now. Unit conversions, concepts from books I read, history, math, geography, trees, plants, programming languages and new languages. The ability to just recognize things and connect concepts without having to look anything up really feels like having a superpower.

The burden of maintaining this knowledge is fairly low, but the habit is really enjoyable. In a given day, I usually don't have more than 20-ish cards that I have to go through and I'm able to get through them while I have my coffee or lunch.

Since introducing this system into my life, I've felt like it's become a little bit of a counterweight to AI. AI's gotten pretty good to the point where you can just let it do its thing and you'll largely come out with a good and/or correct outcome. But you haven't learned anything along the way, nor have you been able to judge if it was the best way to do it. Building stuff with your brain also allows you to build a model of what you're building in your mind. The AI can forget after context compaction or firing up a new thread, but the mind does not forget (as easily) and most importantly, it does not stop. It's always working in the background, making new and novel connections to other ideas and unique experiences in your head.

This has been more of a recent thought for me, that choosing the harder path to memorize things will hopefully better my human output in the long run or at the very least help me grab the reins of an AI as it does its thing.
